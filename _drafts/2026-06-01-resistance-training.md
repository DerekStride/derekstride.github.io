# Resistance Training

## Key Concepts

The 2 key concepts that you have to understand to get effective results in resistance training is Progressive Overload and Deloading.

Progressive Overload can be summed up as increasing the intensity of a workout every session. You have 2 main ways to increase intensity, the first is to increase the weight and the second is to increase the number of reps. You only build muscle and strength when you're training is challenging, by continously increasing the intensity you'll be signalling your body that it needs to adapt.

Deloading is the opposite factor that keeps progressive overload in check. After increasing the intesity week over week, you'll reach a point where the workout will be too challenging to complete. If you don't meet your target rep count that is a sign it's time to deload by decreasing the weight or number of reps. Deloading is a key component to an effective plan & let's your body recover so you can continue pushing yourself in the future.

## Goals & Benefits

There are plenty of benefits to resistance training and it's recommended that adults do 2 resistance training sessions a week.

Among with all the health benefits there are 2 common goals to resistance training, building strength and building muscle. Each have their own training style.

For building strength you want to lift heavy weights for sets with low rep counts. Traditionally, this is 5 or fewer reps.

For building muscle you want to train until failure, that is, you want to do as many reps as possible before you physically can't do more. It's important that they are quality reps, once your form breaks down that's when you should stop. It's also a good to have enough weight where you physically can't do more than 30 reps.

## Measuring Success

As with any measurement, there are [leading and lagging measures](/books/deep-work). The lagging measures are obvious here, it takes a long time to build muscle mass & strength. If you look more muscular or can lift more weight than in the past these are good indicators that your training has been working.

However, tracking leading measures will often lead to improvements in the lagging measures. The weight, reps, & duration of your training are all good leading measurements. Tracking your progress week to week and sticking to the Progressive Overload principles will help motivate you and give you a target to aim for each workout.

## Details

### Why resistance training

Briefly cover the benefits beyond appearance:

- maintaining muscle as you age;
- increasing strength and physical independence;
- supporting bone health;
- improving balance and injury resilience; and
- supporting metabolic and cardiovascular health.

Mention that Canadian guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activity at least twice per week.

### Choose the goal

Different goals put the emphasis on different parts of training:

| Goal | Main emphasis |
|---|---|
| General health | Consistency, full-body training, and sustainable effort |
| Strength | Heavier loads, longer rests, and practising specific lifts |
| Hypertrophy | Sufficient weekly volume and sets close to failure |
| Muscular endurance | Lighter loads and higher repetitions |
| Power | Moving moderate loads quickly with good technique |

### Training variables

Explain the controls available when designing a program:

- **Exercise selection:** which movements are trained.
- **Volume:** how many challenging sets are completed.
- **Load:** how much resistance is used.
- **Repetitions:** how many times the movement is performed.
- **Effort:** how close the set gets to failure.
- **Frequency:** how often each movement or muscle is trained.
- **Rest:** time between sets and recovery between workouts.

### Movement patterns

Organize exercises by movement instead of providing an exhaustive exercise catalogue:

- squat or knee-dominant movement;
- hinge or hip-dominant movement;
- horizontal push;
- horizontal pull;
- vertical push;
- vertical pull;
- loaded carry; and
- core or trunk stability.

A beginner does not need every movement in every session, but the categories show what a balanced program can cover.

### Progressive overload in practice

Give one concrete progression method. Double progression is simple:

1. Choose a range, such as 8–12 repetitions.
2. Keep the same weight while adding repetitions over several sessions.
3. Once every set reaches 12 good repetitions, add a small amount of weight.
4. Return to the lower end of the range and repeat.

Progress is not limited to adding weight or repetitions. Better technique, a larger range of motion, an additional set, or completing the same work with less fatigue can also represent progress.

### Effort and proximity to failure

Introduce **repetitions in reserve (RIR)**:

- **3 RIR:** you could have completed approximately three more repetitions;
- **1 RIR:** you could have completed approximately one more repetition; and
- **0 RIR:** muscular failure.

Distinguish between:

- **muscular failure:** physically unable to complete another repetition; and
- **technical failure:** unable to continue with acceptable form.

Many sets can be productive while stopping one to three repetitions short of failure.

### Fatigue and deloading

Explain what a deload can look like:

- reduce the weight;
- reduce the number of sets;
- keep the exercises but stop farther from failure; or
- take additional rest days.

Possible signs of accumulated fatigue include:

- performance declining across multiple workouts;
- persistent soreness or joint irritation;
- unusual fatigue or loss of motivation; and
- poor recovery despite adequate sleep and food.

One missed repetition does not necessarily mean it is time to deload.

### Measuring progress

Expand the leading and lagging measurements.

**Leading measures:**

- workouts completed;
- exercises, sets, repetitions, and load;
- repetitions in reserve; and
- adherence over weeks and months.

**Lagging measures:**

- increased weight or repetitions at the same effort;
- estimated one-repetition maximum;
- body measurements or consistently taken photos; and
- daily activities becoming easier.

Workout duration is less informative on its own because a longer workout can mean longer rests or additional low-quality volume.

### Claims to revisit

- Progressive overload does not require improvement every session; it means increasing demands over time.
- Missing one repetition does not automatically require a deload; look for accumulated fatigue or repeated performance decline.
- Hypertrophy does not require every set to reach failure; training near failure is generally enough.
- Strength is not limited to sets of five or fewer. Heavy, low-repetition training is specific to maximal strength, but broader repetition ranges still build strength.
- In resistance-training literature, **intensity** usually means load relative to a one-repetition maximum. **Effort** describes proximity to failure.
